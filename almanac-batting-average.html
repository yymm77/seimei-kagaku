What is your almanac's batting average?

A 73-year-old in Tokyo built a Nine Star Ki planner that lets you test it on your own life

Yoshiaki Mihara · October 22, 2026 · 日本語

In baseball, a .300 hitter is a star. He fails seven times out of ten, and we still call him great.

For years I have checked Nine Star Ki directions before important decisions. This week I asked a different question: what is the almanac's batting average on my own life? Not on famous disasters chosen after the fact, but on the ordinary record of where I went and what happened to me. To answer it, I built a planner that imports my calendar and lays the charts beside my past.

A few words on Nine Star Ki

Nine Star Ki (Kyusei Kigaku in Japanese) is an East Asian almanac system. Each year, month and day has a chart of nine stars arranged around a centre, and the stars move through the eight directions in a fixed cycle. Depending on the star you were born under, some directions on a given day are considered favourable and some unfavourable. In Japan, people still consult it before moving house, starting a job or setting out on an important trip.

The planner is called Hōi Yotei-chō, roughly "direction planner." It shows the year, month and day charts for any date, marks the Five Yellow, the Dark Sword and the three Breakers (year, month and day), and colours each day by how it looks for you.

Everyone already has a calendar

When I recommend a new planner to someone, the first objection is obvious: I already have one. Most of us keep plans on a phone, on Google Calendar, often in separate accounts for work and family. Nobody wants to copy them by hand.

So the planner reads your existing calendar. Google Calendar and the iPhone or Mac Calendar can both export plans as an .ics file, the common calendar format. Google gives you a zip file; you can select it as it is, without opening it.

You can bring in as many accounts as you like. Select the zip files from each account together. Each imported plan carries a small tag showing which calendar it came from, such as "Work" or "Family." If the same meeting sits in two accounts, it appears once, and importing again never creates duplicates.

By default the planner imports from one year back. You can also choose three months back, from January of this year, everything, or future plans only. It also works the other way: you can export your plans to a file, or add a single plan to Google Calendar or to an iPhone or Mac with one button.

Why the big calendar apps never did this

Work plans live in a company account, family plans on an iPhone. Our plans end up scattered, and the large calendar services have not gone out of their way to let them move freely between one another. A small tool can reach for exactly that gap.

Test it on your own past

But that is not the main reason I built it.

In Japan I work to preserve a system of name analysis developed by my teacher, under the name "name science." If you call something a science, the person using it must be able to check it. I believe the same should hold for directions: not something to believe or disbelieve, but something you can test.

Once your past year is imported, the day you fell ill, the day you met someone important, the day you passed an exam and the day things went wrong all sit beside that day's chart. Which chart were you under that day? Go through those events one by one, and you can see with your own eyes how well the almanac does.

I would most like people who have no interest in directions to try it. If it does not hold up, that is an answer too. If you feel it does, it is not because someone told you so, but because your own history showed it.

The baseline: 35% by chance

Some time ago, while looking at historical events with Claude, the AI I work with, I learned something sobering. If you pick days and directions at random, about 35% of them land on one of the three Breakers by chance alone. Choose without thinking, and you still "hit" like a .300 batter.

If a prediction is either right or wrong, a coin gets it right half the time. Anyone who could reliably do much better than 50% could make a living on that skill alone. If fortune-telling, directions or the Four Pillars are a kind of statistics, as people often say, then they should be measured with the same ruler.

If directions are statistics, measure this almanac's batting average with your own history. Of the major events in your past, how many happened under an unfavourable chart? How far does that beat the 35% you would get by chance? You are the one who counts.

Almanacs and directions are a guide for preparing your mind. For anything that concerns your health or safety, please consult the proper professionals at once, whatever the day's chart says.

A year view: ten years in a row

The second new feature is a year view. Ten calendar years sit in a row at the top, each coloured by that year's chart for you. The year chart changes at Risshun, the start of spring in early February, not on January 1. For moves that last a year or more, such as moving house, building a home or changing jobs, the year chart matters most. Should you move next year, or wait one more? With ten years side by side, you can compare at a glance. The nine stars complete their cycle in nine years, so ten years show the whole round.

Tap a year, and you see its chart, the reasons for any unfavourable directions, and twelve months below. On a phone, the years scroll sideways in a single row.

How accurate is the calendar?

The solar terms are computed astronomically for 1900 to 2100 and agree with the almanac of Japan's National Astronomical Observatory to within one minute. The month chart changes at each solar term. The direction rules match all 648 cases in the direction table of Akimitsu Takagi, whose Japanese school I follow. Schools differ, and the planner says so.

For this release we checked the year charts for 21 years and the month charts for 252 months against a separately built astronomical calendar, with no differences. We also re-checked 102,483 items on the screen against independent calculations, with no mismatches.

Built with Claude

I am not a programmer. I described the planner I wanted in my own words, and Claude, the AI from Anthropic, wrote the code. I asked for four things on one morning: exchange plans with Google and Apple calendars, go back into the past, gather from several accounts, and show whole years. Those four requests became the features above. Just as important, I insisted that every result be checked against independent calculations. That step caught real mistakes, and it is why I am willing to put numbers in this article.
