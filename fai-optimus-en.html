On Japan's FAI Strategy

The Hand Decides

What Tesla's Optimus V3 tells Japan about its FAI strategy. Public information is set out as fact; my own view is marked and kept separate.

I spent twenty-five minutes watching a video briefing on Tesla's humanoid robot, Optimus.

I once worked at Nissan Motor, in production planning and in the export business. Old habits do not fade. Watching footage of this kind, my mind drifts to bills of materials, production lines, and electricity. It happened again this time. What appeared on screen was a humanoid robot. What was actually being discussed was how many million new parts must be made, in which plant, and on how much power.

So this is not a story about robots. It is a story about industrial structure and national strategy.

In April of this year I submitted a policy proposal to Japan's Cabinet Secretariat and Cabinet Office, titled "FAI, not immigration: Japan already has this answer." FAI stands for Fully Autonomous Intelligence. The world has moved somewhat faster than I assumed when I wrote it. Below I set out what can be confirmed from public information, and then offer my own view.

What Tesla Is Actually Doing

Fact

One. The contest will be decided by the hand, not by walking.

The third generation of Optimus (V3 / Gen 3) carries a hand with 22 degrees of freedom. Structural details were released in February 2026. The essential point is that the actuators driving the fingers sit in the forearm rather than in the palm, and tendon-like cables run through a hollow wrist to move each finger. It is the relationship between the human forearm and its tendons, transposed into machinery.

The hand itself therefore becomes lighter, smaller, and easier to cool. The stated aim is to satisfy two contradictory demands at once: lift a box of more than 18 kg, and pick up an egg or a battery cell without crushing it. What stayed with me most was the admission that this hand will be the single most expensive part of the machine.

Two. The production capacity is being secured before the product is proven.

In its Q1 2026 report, Tesla disclosed that the Model S and Model X lines at Fremont have been converted for humanoid robot manufacturing. The target is on the order of one million units per year, with a second line being prepared at Gigafactory Texas, whose long-term design capacity is described as ten million units per year.

The factory is being rebuilt before anyone knows whether the product will sell. This looks like the wrong order. It is not. It is the judgement of a company that knows exactly how many years mass-production readiness takes.

Three. The electricity is being secured in-house.

The video described Terafab, a semiconductor plant concept in Grimes County, Texas, for which the stated approach is to build natural gas generation and large-scale battery storage rather than depend on the public grid. A company that sells solar chooses thermal generation for its own core plant. This is not ideology. It is the practical judgement of an operator who needs firm power now.

Four. What one unit learns, every unit receives.

If a single machine acquires a new task, the result can be distributed to the entire fleet by software update. Human mastery stays sealed inside one person. Machine mastery replicates. I suspect this is the most fundamental difference embodied in the humanoid robot as a product.

The One-Dollar-Per-Hour Figure

Reported — not confirmed

What follows comes from the video and from various press reports. It is not established fact, and I ask readers to treat it separately.

  • The stated goal is a price below that of a car, with a manufacturing cost of USD 20,000 to 25,000 once annual volume reaches one million units. Initial selling prices are expected to sit above that.
  • At 20 hours a day, 350 days a year, for 10 years, total operating time reaches 70,000 hours. Divide USD 30,000 by 70,000 hours and you get roughly 43 cents per hour. At 0.5 to 0.6 kWh consumed per hour and 15 cents per kWh, electricity costs 8 to 9 cents per hour. The two together come to about 52 cents. Even after allowing the remainder for maintenance, spare parts, and connectivity, the machine works for something near one dollar an hour.

This calculation only carries meaning if three preconditions all hold: the USD 30,000 price, near-continuous operation, and durability measured in tens of thousands of hours. Break any one of them and the number changes substantially. On the Q4 2025 earnings call it was also conceded that no robot is yet performing useful work. The present stage is learning and data collection, nothing more.

Where Japan Stands

Fact

Japan has not been idle.

On 26 March 2026, the inter-ministerial council on AI robotics published Japan's AI Robotics Strategy. Sixteen industrial sectors were designated as targets, and elder care is among them. On 27 May 2026, the government's stated direction for social implementation of AI robotics was revised.

At the centre of METI's approach sits the idea of a "robot-friendly" environment: rather than fitting robots into environments as they are, reshape workflows and facilities so that robots can work in them. This is correct. There are many situations where changing the environment is cheaper and faster than insisting on a humanoid form.

Japan also holds real accumulated capability in components: reduction gears, actuators, force sensors, precision bearings. And it holds the fastest-ageing population in the world, which is to say the largest and most urgent demand that no other country can manufacture for itself.

Five Points for Japan's FAI Strategy

My view

One. Concentrate public investment on the hand, not on locomotion.

Footage of backflips impresses people. It does not wash a dish. Tesla itself concedes that the hand is the hardest and costliest component. And what constitutes a hand is small, high-torque, durable reduction gears and actuators, together with sensors that register touch. This is a field where Japan's component industry can compete directly. Even without winning at the finished-product level, a world in which Japanese parts sit inside the hands of every humanoid robot serves the national interest well enough.

Two. Enter through elder care rather than the factory.

Tesla starts in the factory and moves toward the home. Japan may reverse that order. Three reasons. The urgency of demand here is the highest in the world. Care facilities are enclosed indoor environments, more controllable than outdoor ones. And the data generated there is the most valuable data available.

I am aware of the counterargument. Care work involves direct contact with the human body, so safety standards are far stricter than in a factory. Precisely for that reason, the entry point should not be lifting a person. It should be carrying, patrolling, tidying — the work that surrounds care. The aim is to return to people the work only people can do. The aim is not to reduce headcount.

Three. Design frontline data as a national asset.

If what one unit learns reaches every unit, then in the end volume and quality of data decide the outcome. Which means the questions of how care and maintenance data are standardised, how they are anonymised, and to whom they belong must be settled before the machines arrive. Get the order wrong, and data gathered on Japanese frontlines becomes another country's training asset intact.

Four. Let Japan be first to institutionalise compensation for training data.

The video noted that Optimus can learn by watching online video — cooking, tool use, cleaning. This is where I stopped.

What returns to the person who filmed that dish, to the craftsman who devised that sequence, to the care worker who refined that technique?

The framework I have been proposing, UCA (Universal Creative Attribution), combines SHA-256 hash verification of provenance with automatic compensation through smart contracts. In an era when humanoid robots learn human skill by watching it, such a mechanism stops being philosophy and becomes operational infrastructure. And with norms of this kind, the country that builds one first tends to hold the de facto standard. That, I think, is where the significance lies for Japan.

Five. An FAI strategy without an energy foundation does not stand.

Run one robot at 0.5 kWh per hour, and a million working simultaneously draw 500,000 kW. A Texas company building its own power plants is not making a philosophical statement. It is arithmetic. Japan's FAI strategy, divorced from energy policy, remains a picture of a rice cake. Subsidies for robot adoption and the debate over the generation mix belong on the same table.

"FAI, not immigration" was never meant as a phrase that excludes people. I meant only to say that accepting immigration is not the sole available answer to Japan's labour shortage. Now that a machine working for a dollar an hour has begun to look plausible, the premises underlying that choice are shifting.

Even so, I do not believe technology decides everything. A machine learning to grip an egg without breaking it, and a person being treated as a person, are separate matters.

Beneath the same sun, human beings and machines exist alike. Not ranked above and below, but standing side by side. Whether Japan can receive this technology within that view of the world will, I sense, be what finally divides the outcome.

Granted life, I exist in this moment.
